## Session 1: Lecture — What is Hospital Clinical Data? (15 min)

Clinical data is information generated during the course of patient care. It is fundamentally different from research data in both its origin and its purpose.

**Clinical data vs research data:**

| Aspect | Clinical Data | Research Data |
|--------|--------------|---------------|
| Primary purpose | Patient care delivery | Answering a scientific question |
| Who generates it | Clinicians, nurses, lab technicians, pharmacists | Researchers following a study protocol |
| Collection method | Routine care documentation | Standardized instruments with strict criteria |
| Quality control | Variable — depends on clinical workflow | Rigorous — protocol-driven with audits |
| Completeness | Often missing values (tests not ordered, notes not written) | Designed to be complete per protocol |
| Volume | Massive — every patient, every encounter | Limited — only enrolled subjects |
| Standardization | Varies across hospitals and systems | Uniform within a study |

**How clinical data is generated during a patient encounter:**

The hospital encounter follows a predictable sequence, and each step produces different types of data:

1. **Registration** — Demographics (name, age, sex, insurance), contact information, next of kin. Generated by the admissions desk. Stored in the Hospital Information System.

2. **Triage** — Chief complaint, initial vital signs (blood pressure, heart rate, temperature, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation), acuity level. Generated by the triage nurse. Stored in the nursing documentation system.

3. **Assessment** — History of present illness, past medical history, physical examination findings, differential diagnosis. Generated by the physician. Stored as clinical notes (often free text).

4. **Orders** — Laboratory tests, imaging studies, medications, consultations. Generated by the physician. Stored in the Computerized Provider Order Entry (CPOE) system.

5. **Results** — Lab values (blood counts, chemistry panels, cultures), imaging reports (X-ray, CT, MRI interpretations). Generated by the laboratory and radiology departments. Stored in the Laboratory Information System (LIS) and Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS).

6. **Clinical notes** — Progress notes, nursing assessments, specialty consultations, procedure notes. Generated continuously throughout the stay by multiple clinicians. Stored in the Electronic Health Record (EHR).

7. **Discharge** — Discharge diagnosis (ICD codes), discharge summary, medication reconciliation, follow-up instructions. Generated by the attending physician. Stored across multiple systems (HIS for codes, EHR for the summary).

> **Note:** The primary purpose of all this data is care delivery, not research. When we use clinical data for analysis, we are repurposing data that was collected for a different reason. This has profound implications for data quality, completeness, and interpretation.

## Session 2: Lecture — Hospital Information Systems (20 min)

A modern hospital runs on multiple interconnected information systems. Each system serves a specific function and generates a distinct type of clinical data.

**Hospital Information System (HIS)**

The HIS is the backbone of hospital operations. It manages:

- **Patient registration** — demographics, insurance, unique patient identifiers
- **Admission/Discharge/Transfer (ADT)** — tracks where patients are and when they move
- **Billing** — charges, procedures, diagnosis codes for reimbursement
- **Scheduling** — appointments, operating room bookings

Data generated: patient demographics (age, sex, address), admission dates and times, discharge dates, length of stay, admission type (emergency, elective, urgent), billing codes.

**Laboratory Information System (LIS)**

The LIS manages the lifecycle of laboratory testing:

- **Order receipt** — receives test orders from CPOE
- **Specimen tracking** — tracks samples from collection to analysis
- **Result reporting** — stores and transmits results with reference ranges
- **Quality control** — monitors instrument calibration and proficiency testing

Data generated: lab test results (white blood cell count, hemoglobin, creatinine, HbA1c, etc.), specimen collection timestamps, reference ranges, critical value alerts.

**Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS)**

PACS handles medical imaging:

- **Image storage** — digital X-rays, CT scans, MRIs, ultrasounds
- **Image distribution** — makes images available to clinicians across the hospital
- **Report management** — stores radiologist interpretations

Data generated: imaging studies (structured metadata like modality, body part, date), radiology reports (unstructured text with findings and impressions).

**Pharmacy Information System**

The pharmacy system manages medication use:

- **Order verification** — pharmacists review and approve medication orders
- **Dispensing** — tracks which medications are dispensed to which patient
- **Drug interaction checking** — alerts for allergies, duplicate therapies, dose limits
- **Medication Administration Record (MAR)** — records when nurses give medications

Data generated: medication orders (drug name, dose, route, frequency), dispensing records, administration timestamps, number of medications at discharge.

**Nursing Documentation System**

Nursing documentation captures ongoing patient monitoring:

- **Vital signs** — blood pressure, heart rate, temperature, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation (recorded at regular intervals)
- **Nursing assessments** — pain scores, fall risk, skin integrity, functional status
- **Intake/output** — fluid balance tracking
- **Care plans** — nursing interventions and goals

Data generated: time-stamped vital signs, assessment scores, nursing notes (mix of structured checkboxes and free-text narratives).

> **Note:** In practice, many hospitals use an integrated Electronic Health Record (EHR) system (such as Epic, Cerner, or MEDICOM in Taiwan) that combines several of these functions. However, the underlying data types remain the same regardless of whether they come from separate systems or a unified EHR.

## Session 3: Lecture — Structured vs Unstructured Clinical Data (15 min)

Not all clinical data is created equal. The format of the data determines how easily it can be analyzed.

**Structured data**

Structured data fits neatly into rows and columns. Each value has a defined format and a known set of possible entries.

- Can be directly loaded into a spreadsheet or database
- Ready for statistical analysis and machine learning without extensive preprocessing
- Examples: diagnosis codes, lab values, vital signs, demographics

**Unstructured data**

Unstructured data is free-form text or media without a predefined format. It contains rich clinical detail but requires special processing to extract analyzable information.

- Cannot be directly used in statistical models
- Requires Natural Language Processing (NLP) or manual review to convert to structured form
- Examples: clinical notes, radiology reports, discharge summaries, pathology reports

**Semi-structured data**

Semi-structured data has some organizational structure but also contains free-text elements.

- Parts can be analyzed directly; other parts need preprocessing
- Examples: medication orders (structured drug name and dose, but free-text instructions like "take with food, avoid grapefruit juice"), standardized forms with free-text comment fields

**Examples from a hospital encounter:**

| Data Element | Category | Source System | Example Value |
|-------------|----------|---------------|---------------|
| Age | Structured | HIS | 67 |
| Sex | Structured | HIS | Female |
| Admission type | Structured | HIS | Emergency |
| ICD-10 diagnosis code | Structured | HIS | I50.9 (Heart failure, unspecified) |
| White blood cell count | Structured | LIS | 12.3 (10^3/uL) |
| Hemoglobin | Structured | LIS | 10.8 g/dL |
| Creatinine | Structured | LIS | 2.1 mg/dL |
| HbA1c | Structured | LIS | 8.2% |
| Systolic blood pressure | Structured | Nursing | 148 mmHg |
| Heart rate | Structured | Nursing | 92 bpm |
| Number of medications | Structured | Pharmacy | 11 |
| ICU stay | Structured | HIS/ADT | Yes |
| Discharge summary | Unstructured | EHR | "Patient is a 67-year-old female with a history of CHF and CKD stage III who presented with acute dyspnea..." |
| Radiology report | Unstructured | PACS | "Chest X-ray: Bilateral pleural effusions with cardiomegaly. No pneumothorax..." |
| Medication instructions | Semi-structured | Pharmacy | Drug: Furosemide, Dose: 40mg, Route: PO, Frequency: BID, Instructions: "Monitor weight daily, hold if SBP < 90" |

> **Note:** In this course, we will work primarily with structured data. The hospital discharge dataset you will use contains only structured variables (numeric values, categorical labels, and codes). Understanding that this represents just a fraction of the information in a patient's medical record is important context for interpreting your analyses.

## Session 4: Lecture — Common Data Models (15 min)

Every hospital organizes its data differently. Column names, coding systems, data types, and database schemas vary from one institution to another. This makes multi-site research extremely difficult.

**The problem:**

- Hospital A stores diagnosis as "ICD10_CODE" in one table, Hospital B stores it as "dx_primary" in another
- Hospital A records lab results in mg/dL, Hospital B uses mmol/L
- Hospital A uses a custom severity score, Hospital B uses a different one
- A researcher who wants to combine data from both hospitals must manually map every variable — a process that is slow, error-prone, and often incomplete

**What is a Common Data Model (CDM)?**

A CDM is an agreed-upon standard for organizing clinical data. All participating hospitals transform their local data into the same structure. Once transformed, the data can be queried and analyzed using the same code regardless of which hospital it came from.

**OMOP CDM (Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership Common Data Model)**

The most widely adopted CDM for observational health research:

- **Standardized vocabulary** — maps local codes to a shared set of concepts (e.g., both "DM type 2" and "E11" map to the same OMOP concept for Type 2 diabetes)
- **Standardized schema** — defines tables for persons, visits, conditions, drugs, measurements, procedures, observations, and more
- **Maintained by OHDSI** (Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics) — an international open-science collaborative

**Key tables in the OMOP CDM:**

| Table | What It Contains | Hospital Equivalent |
|-------|-----------------|---------------------|
| PERSON | Demographics (birth year, sex, race) | HIS registration |
| VISIT_OCCURRENCE | Encounters (inpatient, outpatient, ED) | ADT records |
| CONDITION_OCCURRENCE | Diagnoses (ICD codes mapped to SNOMED) | Discharge diagnosis |
| DRUG_EXPOSURE | Medications (mapped to RxNorm) | Pharmacy orders |
| MEASUREMENT | Lab results, vital signs (mapped to LOINC) | LIS results, nursing vitals |
| PROCEDURE_OCCURRENCE | Procedures (mapped to SNOMED/CPT) | Surgical/procedure records |
| OBSERVATION | Other clinical observations | Various |

**Benefits for multi-site research:**

- Write one analysis script, run it at 100 hospitals
- Compare outcomes across institutions and countries
- Larger sample sizes for rare conditions
- Reproducible research — the same query produces the same result structure everywhere

**Real-world example: OHDSI network**

The OHDSI network (https://www.ohdsi.org/) includes over 800 million patient records from databases across 30+ countries. All participants convert their data to OMOP CDM. Studies published using this network have analyzed drug safety, treatment effectiveness, and disease characterization at a global scale that would be impossible with single-hospital datasets.

> **Note:** In this course, we will not work directly with the OMOP CDM. However, understanding why standardization exists helps you appreciate the challenges of clinical data analysis. The hospital discharge dataset we use is already a simplified, standardized extract — in real life, creating such an extract from raw hospital data is a substantial effort.

## Session 5: Hands-on — Explore a Hospital Discharge Dataset (30 min)

In this session, you will walk through a sample hospital discharge dataset and understand each variable: what it represents clinically, where in the hospital it is captured, which system generates it, and what its typical range is.

**The dataset:**

The dataset is a CSV file with one row per patient admission. Each row contains 16 variables capturing demographics, clinical characteristics, laboratory values, vital signs, and the outcome of interest.

**Variable-by-variable walkthrough:**

**patient_id**

- **Clinical meaning:** A unique identifier assigned to each patient at registration. It links all records for that patient across systems.
- **Where captured:** Admissions/registration desk
- **System:** HIS
- **Typical values:** Alphanumeric strings (e.g., P00001, P00002). No clinical interpretation — purely administrative.

**age**

- **Clinical meaning:** Patient's age at the time of admission. Age is one of the strongest predictors of clinical outcomes — older patients have higher comorbidity burden, longer stays, and higher readmission rates.
- **Where captured:** Calculated from date of birth at registration
- **System:** HIS
- **Typical range:** 18-100+ years (adult hospital). Most hospital populations skew older (median 60-70 in many settings).

**sex**

- **Clinical meaning:** Biological sex of the patient. Affects disease prevalence (e.g., heart failure is more common in males), drug metabolism, and reference ranges for lab tests.
- **Where captured:** Registration
- **System:** HIS
- **Typical values:** M (Male) or F (Female)

**admission_type**

- **Clinical meaning:** How the patient entered the hospital. Emergency admissions (through the ED) tend to be sicker and less predictable than elective admissions (scheduled procedures).
- **Where captured:** ADT system at the time of admission
- **System:** HIS
- **Typical values:** Emergency, Elective, or Urgent. Emergency is most common in general hospitals.

**primary_dx_icd**

- **Clinical meaning:** The primary reason for the hospitalization, coded using ICD-10-CM. This is the diagnosis that, after evaluation, is determined to be the principal cause of the admission.
- **Where captured:** Assigned by the attending physician at discharge, often reviewed by medical coders
- **System:** HIS (coding/billing module)
- **Typical values:** ICD-10-CM codes such as E11.65 (Type 2 diabetes with hyperglycemia), I50.9 (Heart failure, unspecified), J18.9 (Pneumonia, unspecified organism). We will cover ICD-10 coding in detail in Meeting 02.

**charlson_score**

- **Clinical meaning:** The Charlson Comorbidity Index, a weighted score that quantifies the burden of comorbid conditions. Higher scores indicate more comorbidities and predict higher mortality. It includes conditions like diabetes, heart failure, renal disease, liver disease, cancer, and HIV/AIDS, each with a weight of 1-6 points.
- **Where captured:** Calculated from the list of diagnosis codes (ICD-10) for each patient
- **System:** Derived from HIS diagnosis data (not directly entered)
- **Typical range:** 0 (no comorbidities) to 15+ (heavy comorbidity burden). Most hospitalized patients score 0-5.

**length_of_stay**

- **Clinical meaning:** Number of days from admission to discharge. A key hospital utilization metric. Longer stays increase cost, risk of hospital-acquired infections, and functional decline.
- **Where captured:** Calculated from admission and discharge dates/times
- **System:** HIS (ADT module)
- **Typical range:** 1-30 days for most acute admissions. Median is typically 3-5 days. Outliers can exceed 100 days (chronic ventilator patients, complex cases).

**wbc (White Blood Cell Count)**

- **Clinical meaning:** Measures the number of white blood cells in the blood. Elevated WBC (leukocytosis) suggests infection, inflammation, or stress. Low WBC (leukopenia) suggests bone marrow suppression, immunodeficiency, or certain infections.
- **Where captured:** Blood draw (venipuncture), analyzed by hematology analyzer
- **System:** LIS
- **Typical range:** 4.5-11.0 (10^3/uL). Values above 11 suggest infection or inflammation. Values below 4.5 suggest immunosuppression.

**hemoglobin**

- **Clinical meaning:** The oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells. Low hemoglobin (anemia) is extremely common in hospitalized patients and is associated with fatigue, dyspnea, and worse outcomes.
- **Where captured:** Blood draw, analyzed by hematology analyzer (part of the Complete Blood Count)
- **System:** LIS
- **Typical range:** 12.0-17.5 g/dL for males, 11.5-15.5 g/dL for females. Values below 7 g/dL typically require transfusion.

**creatinine**

- **Clinical meaning:** A marker of kidney function. Creatinine is a waste product of muscle metabolism filtered by the kidneys. Elevated creatinine indicates impaired kidney function (acute or chronic kidney disease).
- **Where captured:** Blood draw, analyzed by chemistry analyzer (part of the Basic or Comprehensive Metabolic Panel)
- **System:** LIS
- **Typical range:** 0.6-1.2 mg/dL for most adults. Values above 1.5 suggest kidney impairment. Values above 4.0 suggest severe kidney disease.

**hba1c (Glycated Hemoglobin)**

- **Clinical meaning:** Reflects average blood glucose over the preceding 2-3 months. Used to diagnose and monitor diabetes. Unlike a single blood glucose reading, HbA1c is not affected by meals or time of day.
- **Where captured:** Blood draw, analyzed by specialized HbA1c analyzer or chemistry analyzer
- **System:** LIS
- **Typical range:** Below 5.7% is normal. 5.7-6.4% is prediabetes. 6.5% or above is diabetes. Poorly controlled diabetics can have values above 10%.

**systolic_bp (Systolic Blood Pressure)**

- **Clinical meaning:** The pressure in the arteries when the heart contracts. Hypertension (high blood pressure) is a major risk factor for stroke, heart attack, and kidney disease. Hypotension (low blood pressure) can indicate shock or dehydration.
- **Where captured:** Measured by the nurse using a sphygmomanometer or automated blood pressure cuff
- **System:** Nursing documentation / EHR vitals flowsheet
- **Typical range:** 90-120 mmHg is normal. Above 140 mmHg is hypertension. Below 90 mmHg is hypotension.

**heart_rate**

- **Clinical meaning:** The number of heartbeats per minute. Tachycardia (fast heart rate) can indicate fever, pain, dehydration, anxiety, or cardiac arrhythmia. Bradycardia (slow heart rate) can indicate medications (beta-blockers), heart block, or athletic conditioning.
- **Where captured:** Measured by the nurse (pulse check or cardiac monitor)
- **System:** Nursing documentation / EHR vitals flowsheet
- **Typical range:** 60-100 bpm is normal resting heart rate. Athletes may be 40-60. Hospitalized patients are often 70-110 due to illness.

**n_medications**

- **Clinical meaning:** The number of distinct medications the patient is taking at discharge. A measure of medication burden (polypharmacy). Patients on many medications are at higher risk of drug interactions, non-adherence, and adverse events.
- **Where captured:** Medication reconciliation at discharge
- **System:** Pharmacy Information System
- **Typical range:** 0-20+ medications. Elderly patients with multiple chronic conditions commonly take 10-15 medications. Polypharmacy is often defined as 5 or more medications.

**icu_stay**

- **Clinical meaning:** Whether the patient spent any time in the Intensive Care Unit during the admission. ICU patients are critically ill and have higher acuity, higher costs, and higher readmission risk.
- **Where captured:** ADT records (transfer to/from ICU)
- **System:** HIS (ADT module)
- **Typical values:** Yes or No

**readmission_30d**

- **Clinical meaning:** Whether the patient was readmitted to the hospital within 30 days of discharge. This is a standard quality metric used by hospitals and payers worldwide. High readmission rates suggest problems with discharge planning, follow-up care, or disease management.
- **Where captured:** Identified retrospectively by linking the patient's discharge record to subsequent admission records
- **System:** HIS (requires linking across encounters)
- **Typical values:** Yes or No. National average readmission rates are typically 10-20% depending on the patient population.

> **Note:** This dataset is a simplified extract from what would be a much larger, more complex hospital database. In practice, a single patient encounter could generate hundreds of data points across dozens of tables. The 16 variables here represent a curated, analysis-ready subset focused on predicting 30-day readmission.

## Session 6: Lecture — Course Roadmap (15 min)

This course covers hospital clinical data analysis in 17 weeks. The first 9 weeks are lectures that build foundational knowledge. Weeks 10-14 are hands-on sessions using Orange. Weeks 15-17 are for the capstone project and final exam.

**Course schedule:**

| Week | Topic | Format |
|------|-------|--------|
| 1 | Introduction to Hospital Clinical Data Systems | Lecture |
| 2 | Clinical Coding and Terminology Standards | Lecture |
| 3 | Data Quality and Preprocessing in Clinical Datasets | Lecture |
| 4 | Descriptive Statistics for Clinical Variables | Lecture |
| 5 | Data Visualization for Clinical Data | Lecture |
| 6 | Clinical Prediction Models: Concepts | Lecture |
| 7 | Classification Methods for Clinical Outcomes | Lecture |
| 8 | Model Evaluation and Validation | Lecture |
| 9 | Midterm Exam | Exam |
| 10 | Hands-on: Loading and Exploring Clinical Datasets (Orange) | Hands-on |
| 11 | Hands-on: Data Preprocessing and Visualization (Orange) | Hands-on |
| 12 | Hands-on: Building Clinical Prediction Models (Orange) | Hands-on |
| 13 | Hands-on: Model Evaluation and Comparison (Orange) | Hands-on |
| 14 | Hands-on: End-to-End Clinical Analysis Pipeline (Orange) | Hands-on |
| 15 | Capstone Project Work Session | Project |
| 16 | Capstone Project Presentations | Project |
| 17 | Final Exam | Exam |

**How the pieces connect:**

- **Weeks 1-2** (this week and next): Understand where clinical data comes from, how it is coded, and what terminology standards exist. This is the foundation for understanding what the data means.
- **Weeks 3-5**: Learn how to clean, summarize, and visualize clinical data. These are the preprocessing and exploratory steps that happen before any modeling.
- **Weeks 6-8**: Understand clinical prediction models — what they are, how they work, how to evaluate them. This gives you the conceptual framework for the hands-on weeks.
- **Week 9**: Midterm exam covering Weeks 1-8.
- **Weeks 10-14**: Apply everything you learned using Orange. You will load the same hospital discharge dataset, set up variables, preprocess, visualize, build prediction models, and evaluate them — all without writing code.
- **Weeks 15-16**: Capstone project — design and execute your own clinical data analysis using the concepts and tools from the course.
- **Week 17**: Final exam covering the entire course.

**The hospital discharge dataset:**

The dataset introduced in Session 5 will be used throughout the course. You will:

- Explore it in Weeks 1-5 (lectures use it for examples)
- Load and analyze it in Weeks 10-14 (hands-on with Orange)
- Potentially use it (or a variant) for your capstone project

By the end of the course, you will have analyzed this dataset from every angle: understood its clinical context, checked its quality, described its distributions, visualized its patterns, built prediction models, and evaluated their performance.

> **Note:** Each lecture week builds directly toward the hands-on sessions. The concepts you learn in Weeks 1-8 are not abstract — they are the exact steps you will perform in Orange during Weeks 10-14. Pay attention to the "why" behind each topic, not just the "what."
